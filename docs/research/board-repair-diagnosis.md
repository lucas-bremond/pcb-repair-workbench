# Turning expert board diagnosis into guided steps

Professional board-level technicians follow one skeleton with striking consistency: characterize the symptom and its history, inspect with eyes and nose with power off, measure with power off (the input path, then every rail to ground), power up under a current limit and prove the rails, split the board into functional blocks and halve the suspect chain, confirm at component level, then repair, re-run the original failing test and write it down. Two cheap observations do most of the routing between phases: **the unpowered rail-to-ground reading** and **the current drawn at power-up**. Almost every pass or fail threshold in the trade is a comparison against an expected value (a datasheet, a known-good board, an identical twin circuit on the same board) and only a handful of absolute numbers are well sourced, so a guided tool must store where each expectation came from. For the "Investigate" tool, the evidence supports a hybrid design: a short authored opening (intake, safety, power-input checks) followed by a loop that keeps a weight per suspect and failure mode, runs net-level tests that clear whole groups of parts at once, and picks the next step by probability per unit cost for direct checks and by information gain per unit cost for measurements, under hard "do no harm" ordering rules. Every fielded dynamic troubleshooter works this way, and none of them needs a fault database. The evidence has real holes: no published flowchart covers a generic low-voltage board, no field statistics rank component classes, no existing tool derives multimeter steps from a netlist, and the rules for "resets when the motor starts" come from forum answers and one vendor application note. A multimeter also cannot see the millisecond rail dip behind such a reset, so the tool must say "cannot be cleared with a multimeter" as a first-class verdict instead of "pass".

## How to read the sourcing marks

Each threshold and rule below carries a letter. **A** means a vendor, manufacturer, standards or peer-reviewed document that the researchers read in full. **B** means a practitioner source read in full (a shop manual, a trade-magazine article, a training write-up, forum moderators of a repair shop). **C** means a secondary notebook or tutorial of unknown authorship read in full, mostly the anonymous Applied EE notebook, whose numbers are rules of thumb. **D** means the statement was seen only in a search-engine excerpt, so the wording and context are unverified and it must be re-checked before it is hard-coded. **E** means an inference made in this research that no source states. Where two letters appear, the rule is assembled from sources of both kinds.

## Experts converge on one ten-phase skeleton and argue about three points

### The order every source shares

Six independent descriptions of the process (a US Navy training manual, a vendor guide, a trade-magazine article, a tutorial site, a practitioner forum post and a phone microsoldering course) give the same skeleton in the same order. The Navy's six steps are the outer frame: symptom recognition, symptom elaboration, listing probable faulty functions, localizing the faulty function, localizing trouble to the circuit, and failure analysis, which includes repairing, finding what caused the failure, returning the equipment to service and recording it ([NEETS Module 19](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Six-Step-Troubleshooting-Procedure-144.htm), [following page](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Classes-Of-Overhaul-Work-145.htm)). Board-level practice fills in how the power function gets cleared first: define the failure, inspect with power off, measure with power off (rail-to-ground resistance, shorts, opens, leakage), then power-on checks along the power chain, then signals by functional block, then verify and document ([Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)). The trade-magazine version adds that the user's report is "the most helpful 'tool' to start with" and that "the majority of faults on failed boards are major failures such as short or open circuits" ([Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)).

Power comes before signals for a stated reason: "Problems that result in a totally dead unit or affect multiple functions are generally power supply related" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)), and "power faults masquerade as everything else" ([Applied EE notebook, triage](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/triage-and-first-response)). Within the power phase the chain is walked in order (input protection, fuse or zero-ohm link, reverse-polarity device, regulator, local filters, load rails) with a measurement on both sides of every series element, because "a correct voltage at a regulator input does not prove a stable, properly sequenced output" ([Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)). Half-splitting then governs the search inside a chain: the US Army defines it as the technique "which reduces the average number of measurements needed to isolate the faulty stage or component" ([TM 9-254](https://firetrucksandequipment.tpub.com/TM-9-254/css/TM-9-254_231.htm)), and a 16-stage chain needs at most 4 measurements ([Applied EE notebook, fault isolation](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation)). Verification is a phase of its own: repeat the original failure test, confirm normal current draw and stable rails under load, and re-inspect the rework area ([Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)), because, as one field technician puts it, "Burn-in your work - it can go out the next day" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)).

### The three real disagreements resolve into gates

Sources disagree on whether to reproduce the fault under power before inspecting. NEETS and Goldwasser observe the fault first ("Confirm the problem before diving into the repair. It is amazing how many complaints turn out to be impossible to reproduce"), a forum veteran adds "if it's safe to do so", and the trade-magazine author says not to power a damaged board until the cause is found ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm), [EEVblog forum](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/repair/first-step-thou-shalt-check-voltages/msg471527/), [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)). This resolves into a gate, not a choice: reproduce only when intake reports nothing burnt, hot, wet or blown. The second disagreement, "voltages first" against "eyes and nose first", is a matter of wording. The forum thread titled "First Step: Thou shalt check voltages" drew the replies "Before power up. Eyes, nose first" and "Check the voltages _and_ the ripple" ([EEVblog forum](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/repair/first-step-thou-shalt-check-voltages/msg471527/)), and every source that puts voltages first means first among the powered measurements. A tool encodes it as "inspection precedes power, rails precede signals". The third is documentation: two sources start by gathering diagrams and a known-good board, while Goldwasser says "Get used to the idea of working without a schematic" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). The app sits between the two, since its scenario is a partial schematic with a stated confidence per connection. A fourth, smaller conflict concerns how to act on a suspect: Poole's "One Change" rule (replace one component, then test) against a field technician's advice to "'Shotgun' a bad circuit" ([Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). For an unskilled user and a tool that must attribute effects to causes, one change at a time is the only workable rule.

### Decision rules keyed on two observations

No single published flowchart covers the common symptom classes on a generic low-voltage board. The table below is assembled from separate statements that are consistent with one another.

| Observation                                                       | Route                                                                                                          | Basis                           |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |

| Input rail reads about 0 ohm to ground, unpowered                 | Short location: inspect, inject at 1 V or less under current limit, find heat, remove the part, re-measure     | B (repair.wiki, Siborg)         |

| All rails correct on a multimeter, device dead                    | Scope the rails for ripple, then clock, reset, enable, then the signal chain by half-split                     | B (Siborg, Poole)               |

The current-draw rule is the sharpest of these: "Immediate current limiting suggests a short, reverse polarity, a failed power semiconductor, or incorrect assembly" and "Near-zero draw suggests an open fuse, connector problem, disabled regulator, or broken trace" ([Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)). The intermittent rule is the most confidently stated: such faults "are almost always due to bad connections - cold solder joints or internal or external connectors that need to be cleaned and reseated", while problems that fade as the equipment warms "are often due to dried up electrolytic capacitors" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)).

### Priors rank connections first and logic chips last, without statistics

Practitioners agree on the order of suspicion: connections (solder joints, connectors, cables), then electrolytic capacitors, then power components (regulators, diodes, transistors, MOSFETs, tantalum and large ceramic capacitors failing short), with logic ICs last. "The most common physical problems are bad (cold) solder joints, connectors that need to be cleaned and reseated, and bad cables", and component failure "is probably lower on the list of likely causes" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). Dead shorts "are often caused by solder bridges or power components like MOSFETs, diodes, or large capacitors" ([repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)). In consumer electronics more than 5 to 10 years old, electrolytics near heat sources are the first suspect ([Applied EE notebook, field failures](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/component-field-failures), grade C). In devices with mechanisms, "the majority of problems... are mechanical", including a "bad/partially shorted spindle or sled motor" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). History overrides the single-cause assumption: lightning, surge, drop, water and a previous repair produce multiple unrelated faults ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)).

The only number attached to these priors is one contributor's recollection that bad contacts caused "say 75% of all devices I have repaired for the past 16 years" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). It describes through-hole consumer equipment of the 1980s and 1990s and is not a statistic. No published field data ranks component classes for consumer board repair, so the tool's starting weights are ordinal guesses that evidence must be allowed to overturn.

### Safety rules protect the board as much as the person

On a 5 V board the shock hazard sits inside the mains adapter, so the gate is short: unplug and remove batteries ("The device should be physically unplugged, not just 'switched off'"), assume capacitors are charged until measured, and never puncture, bend or pry a lithium cell ([iFixit safety](https://www.ifixit.com/Info/Device_Safety)). "Perform as many tests as possible with power off and the equipment unplugged", connect and disconnect test leads with the equipment unpowered, and when probing live "put electrical tape over all but the last 1/16" of the test probes" ([Goldwasser safety](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/safety.htm)). The last rule exists because "a simple problem can turn into an expensive one due to a slip of the probe" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). Add the board-protection rules: start with low volts and amps and bring both up slowly ([Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)), never fit a larger fuse, and stop if anything smokes, smells or becomes too hot to touch. One rule follows from the sources without being stated by any of them (grade E): when a specific action triggers the fault, each repetition re-applies whatever overstress exists, so repetitions are limited and made with instruments already attached. The example repair's standing warning against pressing Move or Shooting Star repeatedly is this rule.

## Localization runs on comparisons, and each technique has a number and a risk

### Unpowered rail readings: the bands and their exceptions

With the board unpowered and capacitors discharged, a rail is measured to ground in resistance mode and in diode mode, in both probe polarities. The meter's leads are shorted together first, both to prove the meter and to obtain the lead resistance to subtract ([Fluke](https://www.fluke.com/en-us/learn/blog/predictive-maintenance/troubleshooting-dc-power-supply-with-digital-multimeter)). A standard lead pair adds 0.1 to 0.2 ohm ([Fluke 87V MAX manual](https://www.manualowl.com/m/Fluke/87V-MAX/Manual/528870?page=27), D), repair.wiki allows "usually 0.4Ω" ([repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)), and one worked example subtracts 0.3 ohm from a 3.15 ohm reading ([Rossmann forum](https://boards.rossmanngroup.com/threads/820-02098-shorted-ppbus_aon_spkramp_abc_isns-but-nothing-heats-up-even-with-10w-of-power.65597/)). The continuity beeper is never a verdict: Fluke says most meters beep "between 0-50 ohms" ([Fluke](https://www.fluke.com/en-my/learn/blog/electrical/what-is-continuity)), and thresholds quoted for other meters range from about 10 to 200 ohms (manual excerpts, D), so the tool must ask for the ohms value.

| Reading                                              | Verdict                                               | Basis                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Ohms: 0 to 1 ohm, steady, lead resistance subtracted | Hard short, metal to metal                            | C (EE notebook), B (repair.wiki "0Ω")                 |
| Ohms: 1 to 100 ohms, steady                          | Low-resistance path: a component or a resistive short | C (EE notebook). ACDI calls under 10 ohms a short (B) |
| Ohms: 100 ohms to 10 kilohms                         | Possibly normal (pull-ups, termination), compare      | C                                                     |
| Ohms: starts low and climbs toward kilohms or OL     | Normal, capacitors charging. Wait for it to settle    | C, B (Lowne)                                          |
| Diode: 0.000 V in both polarities                    | True short                                            | C, B (iFixit "0 V")                                   |
| Diode: about 0.01 to 0.2 V                           | Partial short, or a legitimately low-ohm rail         | B (one iPhone case at 0.152 treated as shorted)       |
| Diode: 0.2 to 0.3 V in one direction                 | Schottky, or many junctions in parallel. Verify       | C, A (Fluke germanium band)                           |
| Diode: 0.4 to 0.8 V one way, OL the other            | Normal silicon junction                               | A (Fluke 0.5 to 0.8 V), C (0.4 to 0.7 V)              |
| Diode: OL both ways where a junction is expected     | Open line, or no junction on this net                 | B (iFixit)                                            |

These bands come from the [Applied EE notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/measurement/continuity-connections/short-between-nets), [Fluke's diode guide](https://www.fluke.com/en-us/learn/blog/digital-multimeters/how-to-test-diodes), [ACDI](https://www.acdi.com/troubleshooting-electronic-board-power-rail-shorts-with-a-thermal-camera/) and the [iFixit microsoldering course](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem). They carry a standing exception: CPU and GPU core rails legitimately read about 1 to 2 ohms and beep on continuity (one MacBook core rail measured 1.8 ohms on the [Rossmann forum](https://boards.rossmanngroup.com/threads/820-3330-dead-short-on-ppbus.29438/)), and repair.wiki says a non-zero low reading "depends on the voltage and current specifications of the rail". A 5 V consumer board with a small microcontroller has no multi-amp core rail, so on such a board "under about 10 ohms and not climbing" is a fault (grade E). An inductor between two nets also reads near zero and is not a short ([Altium](https://resources.altium.com/p/how-test-short-circuit-pcb)).

Two probe conventions coexist and must not be mixed. For a short check, the black probe goes on ground, because reversing the probes can read low through IC protection diodes ([repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)). For a diode-mode signature, phone technicians put the red probe on ground on purpose and compare the reading (about 0.7 V on a healthy line) with a known-good device ([iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem)). The reason for red-on-ground appears in no source read. The explanation that fits is that it forward-biases the ground-referenced protection diode on nearly every IC pin, giving a finite, repeatable number per healthy pin (grade E). Diode mode passes only about 1 mA and reads OL on a resistive fault that resistance mode catches, so both modes are used (C). Because "a short somewhere is a short everywhere" ([iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem)), the measurement point does not locate the short. Comparing the reading at each IC's bypass capacitor does give a hint, since the lowest reading is closest to the short (C).

### Where the expected value comes from

Technicians judge nearly every reading against a reference: community-posted diode-mode tables, an identical working board, or the twin circuits on the same board ([iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem), [Fluke](https://www.fluke.com/en-us/learn/blog/digital-multimeters/how-to-test-diodes)). The signature-analysis vendor Huntron documents how to work with no good board at all: compare similar pins on the same IC, compare bus lines with each other, and begin at the connectors ([Huntron](https://smtnet.com/library/files/upload/ASA%20white%20paper.pdf)). A relative threshold results: a capacitor reading about twice the ESR of its identical neighbours is suspect even when the absolute value looks fine ([Applied EE notebook, ESR meter](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/measurement/test-instruments/esr-meter), C). Datasheets supply the rest. A DRV8833-class dual H-bridge, for example, fixes its supply range at 2.7 to 10.8 V and its idle current at 1.7 mA typical, 3 mA maximum ([TI DRV8833](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/drv8833.pdf), A). For a one-off board with no schematic, the usable hierarchy is therefore (grade E): a value recorded earlier on the same board when it worked, then an identical circuit on the same board, then the IC datasheet, then the generic bands above. No readable source gives a tolerance for how far a diode-mode reading may differ from its reference before it counts as a fault. Practitioners speak of "the expected value" without a percentage.

### Current draw sorts the fault before any probe touches the board

A bench supply's display, or an inline USB meter, classifies the fault at the input. The one published signature table comes from a single repair shop's manual for phones and laptops: 0.00 A at full voltage means a dead port or controller, a collapsed output with 0.00 A means a main-rail short tripping the charger's protection, 0.01 to 0.05 A means the device detects power but cannot start, pulsing current means a shorted secondary rail, and current that swings as the voltage falls from 5 V to 0 V means a boot loop or reset ([PNW Computers](https://pnw-computers.github.io/IT-Manual/diagnostics/power_diagnostics/), B). The categories generalize and the numbers do not. Adapted to a 5 V board with a microcontroller, LEDs and a small motor (grade E built on B): zero current at full voltage is an open input path, a few steady milliamps with nothing working is a regulator or microcontroller not starting, normal idle with one dead function is a fault local to that function, immediate current limit with collapsed voltage is a main-rail short, normal idle with a jump or dip on one action is a fault in the load that the action switches on, and rhythmic pulsing is a protection circuit retrying or a reset loop. The last case has a datasheet behind it: a DRV8833 that trips its overcurrent limit (2 A minimum, 3.3 A typical) re-enables after 1.35 ms, and "if the fault condition is still present, the cycle repeats" ([TI DRV8833](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/drv8833.pdf), A).

Goldwasser's oldest advice is also the most reusable: record a working unit's current as a "'signature' of health" for later comparison ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). One number per operating mode (idle, LED on, motor running) needs no schematic. Without a bench supply, a multimeter in series serves as the ammeter, and a series resistor or lamp serves as a crude limiter ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). No source gives a rule for choosing the current limit on a small 5 V board beyond "conservative". Setting it just above the known-good idle draw is a sound default (grade E).

### Powered rails: a 5 percent window, measured at the load, blind to dips

Rails are measured at the IC's supply pin, not at the regulator output ([Applied EE notebook, failure modes](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/failure-modes-and-patterns), C). The pass windows are 5 percent: **4.75 to 5.25 V** for a 5 V rail and **3.135 to 3.465 V** for a 3.3 V rail, with as little as 4.35 V allowed at the far end of a worst-case USB cable (all grade D, from excerpts of a [TI reference design](https://ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/TIDUF26/GUID-6FEF7EB9-D30E-40CF-AAB2-676BA10F3841) and a [Microchip characteristics page](https://onlinedocs.microchip.com/oxy/GUID-FAA05C30-286C-48ED-929F-AF87B46D8887-en-US-1/GUID-E7FF3F95-993A-40AE-BAFC-38CA521C035C.html)). Fluke itself gives no percentage and says to compare with the rated output ([Fluke](https://www.fluke.com/en-us/learn/blog/predictive-maintenance/troubleshooting-dc-power-supply-with-digital-multimeter), A). A regulator's symptoms route as follows: no output, check the input, then the output's impedance, then replace it. Output that drops when the load connects means a bad regulator or a partial short. Excess heat means the input is too high or the load is partially shorted ([Purdue ECE 477](https://engineering.purdue.edu/ece477/Course/Lectures/ToSource/Slides/10-Debugging.pdf), C). After the rails come the two checks that make a healthy processor look dead: "If the clock isn't running, nothing downstream will work" and "A processor held in reset looks dead but isn't broken" ([Applied EE notebook, fault isolation](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation), C).

The multimeter's limit is the key fact for the example repair. A multimeter gives a single snapshot and cannot detect transients, ripple detail or intermittent sag, and Fluke recommends an oscilloscope for those ([Fluke](https://www.fluke.com/en-us/learn/blog/predictive-maintenance/troubleshooting-dc-power-supply-with-digital-multimeter), A). The AC-volts mode gives a coarse ripple figure (AC reading divided by DC reading), yet one Fluke model measures AC only to 5 kHz, far below switching and PWM frequencies, and the only numeric multimeter ripple limits found are automotive ([Fluke](https://www.fluke.com/en-us/learn/blog/digital-multimeters/how-to-test-alternator-ripple-voltage-with-a-multimeter), D). No source gives a ripple limit for 5 V or 3.3 V logic rails. The honest tool rule is relative (grade E): record the reading with the load idle and with the load running, flag a large increase, and label any plain DC "rail OK" as an average. Powered probing has its own risks: a slipped probe between fine-pitch microcontroller pins "can be disastrous", a pin is never tied straight to a rail to force a level (always through about 10 kilohms), and a scope probe on a crystal pin "can detune it enough to disable it" ([Purdue ECE 477](https://engineering.purdue.edu/ece477/Course/Lectures/ToSource/Slides/10-Debugging.pdf), C).

### Finding a confirmed short: heat or gradient, and how much current is safe

Once a rail is confirmed shorted, professionals use two families of method. The first feeds the short from a current-limited supply at or below the rail's own voltage and finds what gets warm. The second feeds a small current and follows the voltage gradient to its lowest point. Sources split on the settings, as the table shows.

| Source                                   | Voltage                                                    | Current limit                                                                                  | Basis |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----- |
| ASM International (failure analysis lab) | Ramp slowly from 0 V to the rail's rating                  | Start at 0.2 A. Raise in steps only if nothing passes 40 °C. Keep every part at or below 50 °C | A     |
| ACDI (repair service)                    | The board's rated voltage                                  | Start at 0.5 A, raise 0.5 A at a time. Ignore anything under about 38 °C                       | B     |
| Applied EE notebook                      | 0.5 to 1 V, "keep voltage < 1V"                            | 100 to 500 mA                                                                                  | C     |
| EEVblog members                          | Under 0.5 V, or the rail's voltage                         | 50 mA upward in steps, to 1 A                                                                  | B     |
| repair.wiki                              | "Try to always start at 1V", never above the rail's rating | Never beyond the rail's rating                                                                 | B     |

The settings come from [ASM International](https://static.asminternational.org/EDFA/202102/24/), [ACDI](https://www.acdi.com/troubleshooting-electronic-board-power-rail-shorts-with-a-thermal-camera/), the [Applied EE notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/measurement/continuity-connections/short-between-nets), [EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/testgear/short-finding-on-a-pcb/), [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics) and the [Rossmann forum](https://boards.rossmanngroup.com/threads/820-02098-shorted-ppbus_aon_spkramp_abc_isns-but-nothing-heats-up-even-with-10w-of-power.65597/). The laptop school assumes thick copper planes and shorts of a few milliohms that need amps to dissipate anything. For a 5 V consumer board with thin traces and a small driver IC, the low-current school is the safe default (grade E built on A and B): start at 0.5 V or less and 100 to 200 mA, cap at about 1 A, and stop at 50 °C on any part. Keeping the injected voltage under about 0.5 V means no silicon junction on the board conducts, so the current flows only through the actual short. That matters because logic protection diodes are rated for only about 10 to 20 mA ([Hackaday comment](https://hackaday.com/2022/03/10/short-circuit-tracer-for-a-buck/), B). The damage reports are concrete: 4.2 V injected into a phone rail from a supply that went to 5.16 A produced a pop and smoke, and the damaged part was never identified ([iFixit Answers](https://jp.ifixit.com/Answers/View/593751/Looking+for+a+short+on+VCC+MAIN,+injected+4.2v+blew+component), B). On boards with switching converters, injecting on an input rail before ruling out a shorted high-side switch puts the input voltage on the low-voltage load ([Rossmann forum](https://boards.rossmanngroup.com/threads/820-3330-dead-short-on-ppbus.29438/), B). The supply is set up with its output off, connected, then raised slowly, and the board is never hot-plugged into a live supply ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/testgear/short-finding-on-a-pcb/), B).

Heat detection has a sensitivity ladder: a thermal camera sees a rise of about 2 °C, a finger needs 7 to 10 °C ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/testgear/short-finding-on-a-pcb/), B), and isopropyl alcohol or freeze-spray frost shows where the board warms first ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/beginners/how-to-find-short/), B, and [Techspray](https://techspray.com/using-freeze-spray-to-diagnose-faulty-electronics), A). A dead short on a small capacitor shows at "as low as 50ma", a partial short needs 100 to 200 mA, and a semiconductor with a thermal pad may need 1 A for a few degrees ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/testgear/short-finding-on-a-pcb/), B). Interpretation matters as much as detection: a hot capacitor is probably the fault, a warm inductor or ferrite bead is merely in the current path and is the natural place to split the rail, a warm bare area suggests a solder bridge, and no warm spot at all suggests a short large enough to carry everything applied ([ACDI](https://www.acdi.com/troubleshooting-electronic-board-power-rail-shorts-with-a-thermal-camera/), B). A warm part is in the short path and can be a victim, not the cause (C). Thermal methods fail on very low-ohm shorts. The arithmetic explains the documented failures: a 0.05 ohm short at 0.5 A dissipates about 12 mW, invisible to a finger, while a 2 ohm partial short at the same current dissipates 0.5 W. That gives a selection rule (grade E): above about 1 ohm use heat, below about 0.2 ohm go straight to gradient tracing or part removal.

Gradient tracing needs resolution, not heat. At 0.5 A a meter resolving 1 mV sees about 2 milliohms, and a cheap meter's 200 mV range resolving 100 µV sees about 100 micro-ohms at 1 A ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/beginners/how-to-find-short/), B). A two-wire ohms reading reaches only 10 to 20 milliohms at best, so true milliohm work needs four-wire probing, although watching only the trend relaxes that ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/testgear/short-finding-on-a-pcb/), B). The fallback without instruments is removal: pull parts from the shorted rail one at a time and re-measure, cracked ceramic capacitors first ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/beginners/how-to-find-short/), B). No source gives a measured success rate for any of these methods. iFixit says only that the thermal camera works "in many, but not all cases".

### Opens and intermittents: characterize on four axes, then provoke locally

Intermittents are thermal, mechanical or marginal faults, and the method is to characterize the fault by temperature, position, time and load before provoking it ([Applied EE notebook, triage](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/triage-and-first-response), C). Goldwasser's ordered procedure starts by deciding whether the problem is internal or external, then classifies it as "physical, heat related, or mode related", then inspects the pins of power parts and high-current connectors, prods with an insulated stick, reseats every connector, and only then applies heat and cold spray to selected areas ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm), B). The freeze-spray maker's procedure is to spray isolated areas of the energized unit until it fails, since thermal shock opens brittle joints ([Techspray](https://techspray.com/using-freeze-spray-to-diagnose-faulty-electronics), A). The risks are static charge from the spray, which a wrist strap does not prevent, and condensation that bridges fine traces (A, C). No source gives numbers for tap force, flex limits, spray temperatures or hot-air settings. Two cautions belong in any tool: "Where a problem is found, don't assume there is only one!" and an intermittent often vanishes as soon as troubleshooting begins, "but it will reappear!" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). For nets that carry motor or LED current, a continuity beep proves little, since "continuity alone doesn't prove a reliable connection under current" ([Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)). The stronger check is a voltage-drop test across the net with the load running (grade E).

### Half-splitting a signal chain with a multimeter

Once power, clock and reset are proven, the failing function's chain is listed from input to output and probed at its midpoint: a correct signal there puts the fault in the second half ([Applied EE notebook, fault isolation](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation), C). Before suspecting an IC, its supply, control inputs, clock and data inputs are verified ([Applied EE notebook, field failures](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/component-field-failures), C), and connections come before silicon: "Where one channel is out, suspect a broken wire at the tape head before a bad chip" ([Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)). Digital symptoms map to causes: an output always high or low is a stuck driver, the same value on adjacent pins is a solder bridge, random input values are a floating input or cold joint, and a microcontroller too warm to touch has outputs fighting ([Purdue ECE 477](https://engineering.purdue.edu/ece477/Course/Lectures/ToSource/Slides/10-Debugging.pdf), C). For a motor function the chain is button, microcontroller input, microcontroller outputs to the driver, driver outputs, connector, motor, and the natural midpoint is the driver's input pins (grade E). A multimeter stands in for a scope on steady signals, such as a pin held high while a button is pressed, which makes half-splitting possible without a scope as long as the signal is not PWM (grade E).

### A reset when the motor starts: split the load from the driver first

Three causes compete: a supply dip, noise coupling, or an excessive current demand from a faulty load or driver. The vendor evidence explains the dip. Too little bulk capacitance makes the motor supply move with current demand, the rule of thumb is 1 to 4 µF per watt of motor power, and capacitor ESR matters as much as capacitance: in TI's simulation of a 270 µF case, 0 ohm ESR gave about 100 mV of ripple and 50 milliohms gave about 200 mV ([TI SLVAFT0](https://www.ti.com/lit/pdf/slvaft0), A). A small driver's own thresholds define what "too low" means: a DRV8833 disables its bridge when its supply falls to about 2.5 to 2.6 V, and "a short to ground, supply, or across the motor winding will all result in an overcurrent shutdown" ([TI DRV8833](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/drv8833.pdf), A). These numbers are an example of the class. The example repair's suspect, an MX08L, was not researched and its limits must come from its own datasheet.

The separating tests come from a forum procedure answered by distributor staff: characterize the timing, look for supply droop with a scope because a multimeter misses short dips, and "temporarily power the microcontroller via a battery" to tell a real dip from coupled noise ([DigiKey TechForum](https://forum.digikey.com/t/stm32-based-motor-controller-randomly-resets-when-dc-motor-starts/69814), B, outcome not reported). In one reported case, small decoupling capacitors did nothing, a 1000 µF capacitor across the logic supply cured the resets, and the meter had shown a steady 4.95 V throughout ([Parallax forum](https://forums.parallax.com/discussion/comment/890920), D). The motor itself is hard to judge by ohmmeter: brush contact inflates the reading, as in a 24 V gearmotor that read 0.674 ohm on a multimeter against 0.196 ohm by the voltage-and-current method ([Bodine Electric](https://bodine-electric.com/blog/?p=895), A).

Assembled from least to most invasive, the sequence is this research's synthesis (grade E built on A and B). First, with power off, read the motor supply rail to ground and each driver output to ground and to the motor supply in both polarities, comparing the outputs with each other. Second, unplug the motor and measure its resistance while turning the shaft slowly, since supply voltage divided by that resistance estimates the stall current the supply must survive. Third, power up with the motor unplugged and trigger the function: a reset with no load condemns the driver or the board, not the motor. Fourth, run the motor alone from a separate current-limited supply. Fifth, with the motor connected, capture the rail at the microcontroller's supply pin and the reset pin on a scope in single-shot mode. Sixth, if the rail dips, test the bulk capacitor by ESR or by temporarily paralleling a known-good one, and try a stiffer source. The third step is the highest-value test for a multimeter-only user because it needs no instrument. Substituting a resistor of similar or higher resistance for the motor is the same test in another form. Only the fifth step shows the dip directly. The others bracket it.

## Component tests: what a multimeter proves, and what it cannot

### Four in-circuit rules apply to every part

An in-circuit reading is "the total resistance through all possible paths between the probes" ([Fluke 87V MAX manual](https://www.manualowl.com/m/Fluke/87V-MAX/Manual/528870?page=27), D), which yields four rules. Resistance reads at or below the part's value and capacitance at or above it, so a resistor reading higher than its marking is a real fault while a low reading is ambiguous ([Applied EE notebook, in-circuit testing](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/retro-legacy/testing-and-troubleshooting/in-circuit-vs-out-of-circuit-testing/), C). A low resistance reading is repeated with the leads reversed, and a change reveals a semiconductor junction in parallel (C). The display is watched for several seconds, because "a stable reading is more meaningful than one that changes quickly" ([Siborg, diodes](https://siborg.com/?p=175), B). A leg is lifted only when the reading stays ambiguous, choosing the end least likely to lift a fragile pad (B). By measurement type: electrolytic ESR is reliable in circuit, diode mode on junctions is a usable screen, DC node voltage is always valid, resistance is a rough check, and capacitance is unreliable except on large filter capacitors (C). Every measurement record needs the designator, probe polarity, mode, reading, in-circuit or lifted state and the meter model, since test currents and voltages vary between instruments ([Siborg, diodes](https://siborg.com/?p=175), B).

### Failure-mode distributions say how a part fails, not how often it is the culprit

Military reliability data gives the split of failure modes within a class: film resistors fail open 59 %, drift 36 % and short 5 %. Ceramic capacitors fail short 49 %, drift 29 % and open 22 %. Tantalum chips fail short 57 %, open 32 % and drift 11 % ([DSIAC](https://dsiac.dtic.mil/technical-inquiries/notable/film-resistor-and-capacitor-failure-modes-and-distributions), A). These justify the tests above: check resistors for "high or open" and ceramic and tantalum capacitors for "short". For H-bridge drivers, TI's functional-safety analysis of one automotive part attributes 28 % of die failures to an output stuck shorted to a rail, 16 % to an output stuck open and **30 % to on-resistance that is too high** ([TI SFFS057](https://www.ti.com/lit/pdf/sffs057), A). That last figure carries a warning for the example repair: a driver can pass every static multimeter test and still be the fault. One forum case had an output sitting about 0.5 V low while still driving the motor in both directions ([Pololu forum](https://forum.pololu.com/t/output-voltage-of-mc33926-h-bridge-has-permanently-dropped/5904), D), so the loaded output-to-rail voltage is the test that catches it (grade E). For aluminium electrolytics the sources conflict: a Navy distribution ranks short first at 53 % (D), while the manufacturer Nippon Chemi-Con describes them as "low in catastrophic failures" with wear-out by electrolyte loss (capacitance falls, ESR rises) as the characteristic mode ([Nippon Chemi-Con](https://chemi-con.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Failure-modes.pdf), A). For aged consumer boards the manufacturer's account matches bench experience, and the tool should treat "high ESR" as the leading electrolytic hypothesis.

## Batch sweeps clear whole groups of parts with one reading

No authoritative "standard order of sweeps" is published. The order below is this research's synthesis (grade E) of a trade-magazine article, iFixit's training material, forum practice and the Applied EE notebook, which all describe the same progression: report, visual, unpowered checks, current-limited power-up, thermal localization, then voltage and signal comparison ([Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards), [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem), [Applied EE notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/triage-and-first-response/)).

| #   | Sweep                             | What one pass covers                                                                                                                         | Tools                              | Risk   |
| --- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------ |
| 1   | History and symptom category      | Never worked, stopped working, or intermittent. Sets the priors                                                                              | none                               | none   |
| 2   | Five-senses inspection, power off | Every part against a checklist: bulge, leak, crack, burn, stain, joint, seating                                                              | loupe                              | none   |
| 3   | Meter set-up                      | Lead resistance recorded, beeper not used for verdicts, capacitors discharged                                                                | multimeter                         | none   |
| 4   | Power-entry continuity            | Connector, switch, fuse or polyfuse, series diode, ferrite beads, zero-ohm links                                                             | multimeter                         | none   |
| 5   | Rail-to-ground sweep              | Every power net, ohms and diode mode, both polarities. A clean result marks every shunt part on the net "not shorted"                        | multimeter                         | none   |
| 6   | Semiconductor sweep in diode mode | Every discrete diode, LED, transistor and MOSFET, and power-IC outputs to both rails. Twins compared with each other                         | multimeter                         | none   |
| 7   | Suspect resistors                 | Burnt, power-path, sense and gate resistors, by the "high is real, low is ambiguous" rule                                                    | multimeter                         | none   |
| 8   | ESR sweep of all electrolytics    | The one capacitor measurement that is reliable in circuit                                                                                    | ESR meter                          | none   |
| 9   | First power-up                    | Current first. Stop on smoke, heat or a collapsed rail                                                                                       | bench supply, or adapter with care | medium |
| 10  | Powered voltage sweep             | Input, across the fuse, each regulator's input, enable and output, each rail at the load and at IC pins, reset, then function-specific nodes | multimeter                         | low    |
| 11  | Event-driven tests                | Min/max capture, scope, load substitution, for faults that occur only during an event such as a motor start                                  | scope or bench supply              | medium |
| 12  | Lifting or removing parts         | Last, and only to resolve a reading that earlier sweeps left ambiguous                                                                       | iron                               | high   |

Sweeps 2 to 8 are safe for an unskilled technician and need no power. The rail-to-ground sweep is the most economical measurement in the whole process because all capacitors on a rail are in parallel: one short makes the whole bank read shorted ([PNW Computers](https://pnw-computers.github.io/IT-Manual/diagnostics/capacitor_testing/)), and conversely one healthy reading clears "shorted" for every one of them. It does not clear open or high-ESR capacitors, a dead IC that is not shorted, or an intermittent short. Twin comparison is the sweep that substitutes for a golden board: identical diode channels, identical capacitors in a bank, pins of the same type on one IC ([Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=175), [Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2023/12/14/hacker-tactic-internal-esd-diode-probing)). The five-senses pass carries one firm rule: "If anything looks or smells burnt, don't power it up again until the cause is understood" ([Applied EE notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/triage-and-first-response/), C).

## Guided-diagnosis tools and procedure standards point to a hybrid design

### No existing board tool reasons about the next measurement

Board repair software is reference material. The boardview tool FlexBV shows per-net expected diode, voltage and resistance values from a community database, highlights which nets have reference data, and expands a net through fuses, inductors and low-value resistors up to three levels deep ([FlexBV manual](https://pldaniels.com/flexbv5/manual/flexbv-manual.html)). Repair wikis order causes per symptom: on an iFixit problem page each heading "should be written as a cause rather than a solution", with "more probable (or more-easily-eliminated) causes generally coming first" ([iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/Info/Problem_Page)). A retro-computing wiki demands that every action name its test point and expected value: "'Measure +5 V at pin 8 of the 6502' is useful; 'check the power supply' is not" ([RetroTechCollection](https://retrotechwiki.com/Help:Writing_a_repair_guide)). Automated testers compare a suspect board with a golden one ([Huntron](https://smtnet.com/library/files/upload/ASA%20white%20paper.pdf)). A 2026 language-model workbench highlights pins and traces nets from schematic and boardview files, which remain rare ([Fight to Repair](https://fighttorepair.substack.com/p/how-ai-is-set-to-transform-electronics)). None of these leads a technician step by step while tracking suspects from the board's own netlist. The app already holds what the reference viewers hold, plus a confidence per connection, so the new value is the reasoning layer.

### Every industry wraps diagnosis in the same envelope

Aviation, automotive and medical-device service all use one envelope around the isolation steps: entry by symptom or code, prerequisites and safety, confirm the fault exists, branch on observations, fix, confirm the repair. A public S1000D sample shows the node structure: an action (check the pressure with the tire pressure gauge), a question, and labelled answer ranges that each name the next step, with three numeric ranges in one node and a yes or no in another ([S1000D sample](https://www.docuneering.com/s1000d/demo/pdf/DMC-S1000DBIKE-AAA-DA0-10-20-00AA-400A-A_007-00_EN-US.pdf)). A Boeing fault isolation task opens with an Initial Evaluation to "verify that the fault condition still exists before you start", declares an intermittent when it cannot be confirmed, and requires repair confirmation after every corrective action, according to a third-party summary that contradicts itself on whether step order is mandatory ([AviationHunt](https://aviationhunt.com/?p=6844)). Two phases of this envelope are the ones hobby repair skips: confirming the fault before isolating it, and confirming the repair afterwards.

Authored trees fail in a characteristic way. Volkswagen's own bulletin on its guided fault finding lists fault codes with "no test created in current test plans", a test step that declares a voltage "is OK" although the starter did not turn, and a step that names a different module from the wiring diagram ([VW bulletin via NHTSA](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2022/MC-10209102-0001.pdf)). The HP printer troubleshooter's designers rejected decision trees for this reason: every allowed sequence must be encoded by hand, giving "longer troubleshooting sequences with lower probability of actually diagnosing the problem" ([US patent 6,535,865](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6535865)). The alternative they built, which survives commercially as Dezide, "weighs failure probabilities, repair costs, and everything the technician has answered, then calculates the best next step" ([Dezide](https://www.dezide.com/)). Its ancestor was built so "that any user, however, inexperienced, should be able to repair the failed equipment on his own" ([Langseth and Jensen 2003](https://www.fs.isy.liu.se/Edu/Courses/DocDiagnos/CourseMaterial/troubleshooting_coherent_systems.pdf)).

### Procedure-writing standards agree on the shape of a step

A novice needs what aviation calls a do-list, in which "the checklist is used to 'lead' and direct" each action, as opposed to a checklist that verifies work done from memory. Its weakness is that "a mistake can easily pass unnoticed once the sequence is interrupted" ([Degani and Wiener 1990, NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19910017830/downloads/19910017830.pdf)). The same report gives the rule that shapes the response field: "Checklist responses should portray the desired status or the value of the item being considered (not just 'checked' or 'set')". It also says the most critical items go first, to raise the chance of completing them before an interruption, and long lists are split into chunks tied to systems and functions.

The US Department of Energy's writer's guide (cancelled in 2015 in favour of an industry standard, and still a clear statement of the practice) supplies the grammar: "Start the basic action step with a singular present tense action verb", identify equipment "precisely as it is in the facility", "Describe the condition first and then the action", state acceptance criteria at the step as a value with a range ("35 psig (30 to 40 psig)"), provide space for entering data, and place warnings "immediately before and on the same page as the action step(s) to which they apply", written as statements of the hazard and its consequence, with no action inside them ([DOE-STD-1029-92](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/308015)). iFixit's reported preference for warnings after the action "when possible" conflicts with this. For anything that can damage the board or the person, before is right, because a novice acts on the first thing read. Wizard research adds the interface rules: wizards suit novices and infrequent tasks, must show the list of steps and the current position, allow saving and resuming, make each step self-sufficient, and keep explanations beside the step ([Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wizards/)). iFixit adds that a novice's time estimate is an expert's plus 20 to 50 percent ([iFixit Tech Writing Handbook](https://about.ifixit.com/Tech_Writing/chapter/8)).

Together these fix a step template for the Investigate tool (the template is grade E, each element is sourced above). It opens with the preconditions as state the technician ticks: power on or off, adapter unplugged, meter mode and range, which probe goes where. A hazard statement follows only when one applies, before the action. Then comes one imperative action naming the part and pin exactly as the board view shows them, with both probe points highlighted on the photo, which is the software form of the checklist advice to touch the control. Then the expected result with a unit and a range, and the origin of that expectation. Then the response: the measured value itself, plus explicit non-answers ("could not measure", "reading unstable", "not sure", "skip for now", "this step looks wrong"). One line says why the step is asked, naming the suspects it separates. The step closes by showing what the tool concluded and how the suspect list changed, with undo.

## Simple rules choose the next test, and they map onto the app's model

### Three research traditions give one rule

The decision-theoretic troubleshooting literature, the test-sequencing literature and model-based diagnosis agree: keep a probability per suspect, and at each step take the action with the best expected benefit per unit cost, recomputing after every answer. For a check that can find or fix the fault, the minimum expected cost comes from "ordering the components according to a decreasing probability to cost ratio" ([Breese and Heckerman 1996](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.3563), A). For a pure information test, "the best measurement is the one which minimizes the expected entropy of candidate probabilities", a one-step lookahead whose practical form is to take the first reasonable measurement that "on average, splits the candidate space in half" ([de Kleer and Williams 1987](https://www.fs.isy.liu.se/Edu/Courses/DocDiagnos/CourseMaterial/deKleer_Williams_1987.pdf), A). Weighting by cost gives information gain per unit cost ([Wahl and Sheppard 2010](https://www.cs.montana.edu/sheppard/pubs/phm-2010.pdf), A). Half-splitting is the special case of equal probabilities and equal costs. A question is worth asking when its value of information exceeds its cost ([Langseth and Jensen 2003](https://www.fs.isy.liu.se/Edu/Courses/DocDiagnos/CourseMaterial/troubleshooting_coherent_systems.pdf), A).

Three refinements from the fielded systems matter for a bench tool. Cost is a weighted sum of time, risk of breaking something else, money and "insult" (a step too basic for the user), and in HP's example weights "a 0 minute step with very high risk is equal to a 72 minute step with very low risk" ([Skaanning 2000](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3893), [US patent 6,535,865](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6535865)). Operator error is modelled: each step's evidential effect is multiplied by the probability it was done correctly ([Skaanning 2000](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3893)). Unknown causes are lumped into a single "all other" fault repaired by a service call ([Breese and Heckerman 1996](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.3563)). All of these rankings are greedy and exact only under a single-fault assumption. One worked example shows a greedy sequence costing 1.58 against an optimum of 1.47, and the remedy offered is simply "to recalculate the efficiencies each time new evidence comes in" ([Langseth and Jensen 2003](https://www.fs.isy.liu.se/Edu/Courses/DocDiagnos/CourseMaterial/troubleshooting_coherent_systems.pdf)). With a few dozen suspects on one board, the theoretical hardness of optimal sequencing is irrelevant.

### The rules, stated for implementation

Everything in this subsection is this research's assembly (grade E). Each ingredient is cited above, and no published tool or paper derives multimeter test steps from a reverse-engineered netlist, so the assembly needs validating on the example board.

**The state is a weight per suspect and failure mode.** A suspect is a part with a failure mode (shorted, open, drifted or high-resistance, functionally dead), a joint or connector, a net-level fault (a short between two nets), or the explicit catch-all "something not in the model". One flag per part is not enough, because the rail-to-ground sweep clears "shorted" and nothing else. Starting weights are ordinal: connections and connectors highest, then electrolytics (high ESR), then load-switching power parts and regulators, then ceramic and tantalum capacitors as shorts on a rail, then logic ICs. They are raised for parts on the nets the symptom touches, falling with distance through series elements, and raised again by intake answers (liquid, drop and earlier repair flatten the weights, since the single-cause assumption no longer holds). The app's existing suspects list in the repair log becomes the visible face of this state. Its existing statuses carry over, and each suspect gains a failure mode, a weight shown as a rank or a bar and never as a certainty, and links to the observations that moved it.

**Tests are generated from the scenario, at two levels.** Net-level tests come from the netlist: each power net to ground (one reading per net, clearing "shorted" for every shunt part on it), continuity along the power-entry path, continuity between two pins the model puts on one net, the voltage on each rail at each load pin, and compare-the-twins tests wherever the netlist shows identical parts wired identically (two driver outputs, parallel LEDs, a bank of decoupling capacitors). Part-level tests are the existing "Test this part" protocols, which already hold steps, typed readings and an outcome. Each test declares which suspects it speaks about and what each predicts (fail, pass, or nothing), which is the dependency-matrix idea from the test-sequencing literature, where the fault-test matrix "can be computed by the reachability analysis algorithms" from connectivity ([Pattipati et al. 1997](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19980096374/downloads/19980096374.pdf), A). Splitting tests come from series elements: any two-pin part joining two non-ground nets (fuse, ferrite, inductor, zero-ohm link, connector) divides the suspect set when lifted or unplugged, and unplugging a connector is far cheaper and safer than desoldering.

**Hard ordering rules come before any scoring.** These are constraints, not costs, following the checklist rule that critical items go first. The safety gate and intake precede everything. Unpowered sweeps precede any powered step. The rail-to-ground sweep on a net precedes applying power to it. The adapter is measured before it is connected. A step that repeats the fault-triggering action is rationed and offered only with the measuring instrument already attached. Injection is offered only when the technician has declared a bench supply, and only after the voltage limit (at or below the rail's nominal, 0.5 V by default), the starting current and the 50 °C stop are confirmed on screen. Lifting or removing a part is offered only when every no-risk and low-risk test that separates the same suspects is done or deferred. A step whose required instrument was not declared is never offered. Its place is taken by the best fallback, together with a statement of what the fallback cannot show.

**Scoring among the allowed steps is benefit per cost.** Cost is minutes plus a risk surcharge by tier (none, low, medium, high) plus a small penalty for a tool change, with the risk surcharge large enough that desoldering never outranks an unpowered measurement that separates the same suspects. For an information test, the benefit is how evenly its outcomes split the current suspect weight: a test whose pass and fail outcomes are equally likely is worth the most, and one whose outcome is nearly certain is worth almost nothing. For a direct check or replacement of one suspect, the benefit is that suspect's probability. The simple switching rule: prefer information tests while no suspect holds more than about half the weight, then switch to direct checks of the leader. Ties break toward tests that also verify a low-confidence connection, toward tests in the same board area and meter mode as the previous step, and toward tests that need no extra instrument.

**Updates are soft, and they respect what the app knows about its own model.** A result multiplies each suspect's weight by a factor: high when the result is what that suspect predicts, low when it contradicts it, and one when the suspect predicts nothing. The weights are then renormalized. The notes' placeholder factors of 0.9 and 0.1 are not evidence: no study of how often novices misread multimeters was found. Soft updates keep cleared suspects recoverable, which the single-fault assumption requires, since it "will run (albeit approximately) for a device with multiple faults" ([Breese and Heckerman 1996](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.3563)). When a test's reasoning passes through a connection marked `assumed`, `datasheet` or `photo`, the factor is flattened toward one, because a net is only as certain as its weakest connection, and when such a connection gates many conclusions the tool proposes verifying it first. A passed continuity test between two pins the model puts on one net is also evidence for the model and promotes that connection to `measured`, with the reading as its evidence. A failed one is ambiguous between an open on the board and a wrong model, and the tool says so. Pins not on any net are unknown, so "every part on the rail" is a lower bound, and parts whose supply pin is unmapped are listed as "not covered" by a net-level pass. For an intermittent fault a passing test only lowers a weight, following de Kleer's treatment in which a faulty component behaves correctly on a given observation with some probability ([de Kleer 2009](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2009/127)). Non-answers leave the weights unchanged and mark the step deferred. Deferred steps return only when untried steps run out, as in the HP system. When a single reading would clear or convict a strong suspect, the tool asks for a repeat or a cross-check on a twin first.

**End states are explicit.** A suspect is confirmed by an out-of-circuit test, a substitution, or a removal that makes the fault disappear, never by weight alone. Parts in parallel between the same two nets form an ambiguity group that no in-circuit meter test can split, the formal term for diagnoses that "no tests exist capable of differentiating" ([Wahl and Sheppard 2010](https://www.cs.montana.edu/sheppard/pubs/phm-2010.pdf)). The tool presents them as a group and offers the only splitters left: lift one leg, a thermal check under injection, or replace the cheapest. When the catch-all dominates or the allowed tests run out, the tool says so, summarizes what was cleared and falls back to free-form logging. After a fix, the confirmation test from the session's opening is re-run over several cycles. If the symptom remains, the loop continues with the repaired part removed from the suspect group, the test-and-repair cycle of the multiple-fault literature ([Pattipati et al. 1997](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19980096374/downloads/19980096374.pdf)). The expert path costs little: show the ranked candidates, let the user pick any of them, enter a result for a test the tool did not ask for, or assert a conclusion with a note, all through the same update and log.

### What this means for the example repair

The planetarium's log already rules out the adapter and the motor and holds the motor driver U4 and the 47 µF electrolytic beside it as the prime suspect. The research supports the planned order of work and sharpens it. The authored opening for "resets when a load starts" is the six-step sequence above, of which the unpowered output-to-rail comparison of the driver's twin outputs and the load-disconnected split need only a multimeter. A static pass on U4 does not clear it, given the 30 % share of high on-resistance failures in the one published driver distribution, so the loaded output voltage and the supply voltage at U4's pin during a start are the deciding measurements. A multimeter-only user gets an honest statement that the millisecond dip is invisible without a scope or a min/max capture mode. The 47 µF capacitor cannot be cleared with a multimeter at all: the valid tests are an ESR reading against the chart (about 1 to 3 ohms for that value at low voltage ratings) or temporarily paralleling a known-good capacitor with correct polarity. Each powered start counts against a small ration, which is the log's existing warning expressed as a rule.

### What to record

Sources agree on the content of a repair record and publish no template. NEETS ends with recording "the necessary information in a recordkeeping book for other maintenance personnel". Siborg lists the failed condition, measurements, corrective action, replacement part and final verification, and says to "preserve evidence before rework". Poole keeps a notebook of voltages against the manual's values. The Applied EE notebook writes the prediction before the measurement ([NEETS](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Classes-Of-Overhaul-Work-145.htm), [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171), [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php), [Applied EE notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation)). Formal failure-reporting standards are organizational, yet their skeleton scales down to one board: report, verify the failure, analyse, correct, verify the correction, close out. The record model below is this research's synthesis (grade E), sized for one board and aligned with what the app already stores.

| Record      | Fields                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Where it lives in the app today                                                                      |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

Three properties matter more than the field list. Observations are immutable and corrections are new entries that supersede, following laboratory-notebook practice of keeping raw data apart from analysis and never erasing it. Scores are recomputable from the observation list and the rules, which gives undo, audit and "what if this reading was wrong" without extra machinery, and it is what the HP system handed to a human expert on failure: the log of steps and the final cause probabilities ([US patent 6,535,865](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6535865)). The interrupted do-list problem means the session persists its exact place and the board state it assumes (powered or not, motor unplugged, parts lifted) and restates that state on resume.

Two source-access notes affect confidence. ### What was not found

No complete flowchart for a generic low-voltage board is published. The decision table in this report is assembled from separate statements. No field statistics rank component classes by how often they are the culprit in consumer repair: the military figures give mode splits within a class, and the 75 percent bad-contact figure is one person's recollection. The leading video practitioners (Rossmann, NorthridgeFix, Learn Electronics Repair, Electronics Repair School, Mend It Mark) publish mainly as video and are represented only through forum posts and a community wiki. No vendor document gives a multimeter-only troubleshooting sequence for a buck converter, a power-off signature for a healthy H-bridge driver, a ripple limit for logic rails, a diode-mode tolerance against a reference, the expected DC voltage on crystal pins, typical winding resistance for small toy motors, or a rule for the current limit on a small board. No source treats "resets when a motor starts" as a repair diagnosis as opposed to a design problem. In a repair the board once worked, so a degraded part is implied, and that reasoning is this research's own. The MX08L driver was not researched. No measured success rate exists for any short-location method. No published tool or paper derives multimeter steps from a hand-built netlist, no study compares static trees with dynamic troubleshooters in electronics repair, and no data says how often novices misreport readings. The standard for rework workmanship, IPC-7711/7721, governs how a repair is executed and prescribes nothing about diagnosis. Model-based reasoning about what voltage to expect at an arbitrary analog node needs component behaviour models the app does not have. Only topology-level reasoning is supported by what was found.

## Conclusion

The research changes the picture of what an "Investigate" tool is. It is less a wizard that knows the answer than a bookkeeper that knows the order of safe questions, and its three hardest duties are honesty about expectations, honesty about instruments and honesty about its own model. Expectations: on a one-off board nearly every pass criterion is a comparison, so the provenance of each expected value deserves the same ladder the app already applies to connections, and "no reference" is a legitimate thing to display. Instruments: the most common powered symptom on small boards, a reset under load, is exactly the one a multimeter averages away, so verdicts need a third value beside pass and fail that means "not testable with what you have", with the bracketing tests (unplug the load, substitute it, parallel a capacitor) offered in its place. The model: every continuity and rail measurement the technician makes for diagnosis is also evidence about the netlist, so an investigation that ends without finding the fault still leaves the scenario more `measured` than it began, and the next investigation starts from better ground.

The untested part is the engine itself. The scoring rules are sound in the literature and unproven on a reverse-engineered board with many unknown pins, and the update factors are placeholders. The example repair is the natural trial: replay its fifteen logged investigation rows through the rules and check that the engine would have proposed the adapter check, the motor-unplugged split and the driver's twin-output comparison in roughly the order a careful technician chose them, and that it never proposes a step the log's warning forbids. Where it disagrees with the log, the disagreement is the most valuable result, in the same way that a contradicted prediction is the most valuable measurement at the bench.

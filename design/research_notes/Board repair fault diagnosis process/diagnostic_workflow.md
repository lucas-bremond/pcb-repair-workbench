# Diagnostic workflow of professional board-level repair (process and order)

Scope: low-voltage consumer and embedded boards (USB or DC-adapter powered, 3.3 V / 5 V logic, small motors, LEDs, microcontrollers), one person with hand tools. Research date: 2026-11-22.

How to read the citations: a plain link means the page was read in full. "(search snippet only)" means only the search engine's excerpt was seen, so the wording and context are not verified. Two primary sources could not be fetched live and were read from Internet Archive copies: Goldwasser's FAQ (repairfaq.org did not resolve) and repair.wiki (HTTP 403). The canonical URLs are cited.

Equipment tags used below: [DMM] multimeter only, [PSU] current-limited bench supply, [THERMAL] thermal camera, [SCOPE] oscilloscope, [ESR] ESR or LCR meter, [REF] known-good board or published readings.

## 1. What are the canonical phases, in which order, and where do experts disagree?

### Takeaway

Every source read converges on the same skeleton: characterize the symptom and history, inspect with power off (eyes and nose), measure with power off (input path, rail-to-ground resistance), power up under current limit and check rails, split the board into functional blocks and localize, confirm at component level, repair, then re-run the original failure test and write it down. The disagreements are about three points only: whether the fault is reproduced under power before or after inspection, whether voltages or visual inspection is "step one", and how much documentation (schematic, known-good board) is required before starting.

### Cited Findings

Phase order as stated by each source (each row is that source's own order):

| Source                                                           | Order of phases given                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| US Navy NEETS Module 19 (training manual)                        | 1 Symptom recognition, 2 Symptom elaboration, 3 Listing probable faulty functions, 4 Localizing the faulty function, 5 Localizing trouble to the circuit, 6 Failure analysis (identify part, repair, find the cause, return to service, record)                                                                                                                               |
| Siborg "Guide to Printed Circuit Troubleshooting" (vendor, 2026) | 1 Define the failure, 2 Power-off visual inspection, 3 Power-off measurements (rail-to-ground resistance, shorts, opens, leakage, in-circuit tests, signature comparison), 4 Power-on checks (power chain, current draw, regulator outputs on a scope, signal path by functional block), 5 Verify the repair, then document                                                   |
| Alan Lowne, Nuts & Volts "Repairing Circuit Boards"              | 1 Failure history from the user, 2 Do not power a damaged board, 3 Remove conformal coating at test points, 4 Prepare bench (diagrams, known-good board, instruments), 5 Visual inspection, 6 Locate shorts, 7 Controlled power-up, 8 Simple powered tests (rails, regulator outputs, clocks, logic levels), then signal probing, comparison with a good board, intermittents |
| Ian Poole, Electronics Notes "Systematic Fault Finding"          | 1 Obvious issues first, 2 Look inside and follow the signal paths, 3 Draw a block diagram (power supply, input and processing, output), 4 Isolate the failing section by measuring at block boundaries, 5 Progressive tracking down to components                                                                                                                             |
| G0HZU, EEVblog forum (practitioner, 2014)                        | 1 Confirm the unit is really faulty, 2 Gather fault history, 3 Observe under fault conditions if safe, 4 Read manual and schematics, 5 Disassemble, logging notes and photos, 6 Look and sniff, 7 Follow the flowcharts or create your own                                                                                                                                    |

- NEETS introduces its procedure with "Basically, any repair of electric or electronic equipment should be done in the following order", then: "Symptom recognition. This is the action of recognizing some disorder or malfunction in electronic equipment." "Symptom elaboration. Obtaining a more detailed description of the trouble symptom is the purpose of this step." "Listing probable faulty functions. This step is applicable to equipment that contains more than one functional area or unit. From the information you have gathered, where could the trouble logically be located?" "Localizing the faulty function. In this step you determine which of the functional units of the multiunit equipment is actually at fault." Source: [NEETS Module 19, The Technician's Handbook, p. 1-134](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Six-Step-Troubleshooting-Procedure-144.htm)
- NEETS steps 5 and 6: "Localizing trouble to the circuit. You will do extensive testing in this step to isolate the trouble to a specific circuit." "Failure analysis. This step is multipart." The technician identifies the faulty part, repairs or replaces it, determines what caused the failure, must "return the equipment to its proper operating status", and "record the necessary information in a recordkeeping book for other maintenance personnel". Source: [NEETS Module 19, following page](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Classes-Of-Overhaul-Work-145.htm)
- Siborg phase 1 (define the failure): record what the assembly should do and how it fails, and whether the fault is constant, intermittent, temperature-dependent, load-dependent or mode-specific. Establish reference conditions (input supply, polarity, current limit, connectors, firmware). A working board is "one of the most useful diagnostic references." Source: [Siborg, Guide to Printed Circuit Troubleshooting](https://siborg.com/?p=171)
- Siborg phase 2: inspect under magnification before applying power (missing or misoriented parts, solder bridges, lifted pads, weak fillets, damaged vias, contamination, connector wear, cracked MLCCs, discoloured parts), check polarity and pin 1, check mechanically stressed areas (board-edge connectors, heavy inductors, large capacitors, mounting holes). Appearance alone is not proof of a fault. Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)
- Lowne: the user report is "the most helpful 'tool' to start with"; visual inspection is "an essential first step"; do not power a damaged board at first, and if a fuse blew, find the cause instead of fitting a larger fuse; "Start with low volts/amps and bring both up slowly"; "The majority of faults on failed boards are major failures such as short or open circuits." Source: [Nuts & Volts, Repairing Circuit Boards](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)
- Goldwasser's rule on reproducing first: "Confirm the problem before diving into the repair. It is amazing how many complaints turn out to be impossible to reproduce or are simple cockpit error. It also makes sense to identify exactly what is and is not working so that you will know whether some fault that just appeared was actually a preexisting problem or was caused by your poking. Try to get as much information as possible about the problem from the owner." Source: [Goldwasser, Troubleshooting and Repair of Consumer Electronic Equipment, v2.50](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Goldwasser on thinking before measuring: "Don't start with the electronic test equipment, start with some analytical thinking." and "Your built in senses and that stuff between your ears represents the most important test equipment you have." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Goldwasser on common cause: "Realize that coincidences do happen but are relatively rare. Usually, there is a common cause... first look for a common root cause rather than trying to locate bad parts in separate circuits." Stated exceptions: "lightning, power surge, dropped, water, or previous repair person damaged equipment." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Disagreement, voltages first versus inspection first: the EEVblog thread "First Step: Thou shalt check voltages" opens with "When you first start troubleshooting, check the voltages!" (Tinkerer, 2014-06-29). Replies put inspection earlier: "Before power up." "Eyes, nose first." (pickle9000), "Before I start any kind of voltage checks I like to give as thorough of an inspection that I can", including reseating connectors and cleaning contacts (at2marty), and "Check the voltages _and_ the ripple." (David Hess). Source: [EEVblog forum](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/repair/first-step-thou-shalt-check-voltages/msg471527/)
- Disagreement, powering a faulty unit to observe it: NEETS, Goldwasser and G0HZU observe the fault first (G0HZU qualifies it with "if it's safe to do so"), while Lowne says not to power up a damaged board until the cause is found. Sources: [NEETS](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Six-Step-Troubleshooting-Procedure-144.htm), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm), [EEVblog forum](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/repair/first-step-thou-shalt-check-voltages/msg471527/), [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)
- Disagreement, documentation: Lowne and Siborg start by gathering diagrams and a known-good board (without them "the challenge [is] more daunting"). Goldwasser says "Get used to the idea of working without a schematic", and a contributor to his FAQ writes "If I need a schematic, then I'm really desperate". Sources: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards), [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Disagreement, one change versus shotgun: Poole's "One Change" rule is to replace one component at a time and test after each change. Roger Pariseau, in Goldwasser's FAQ, lists "'Shotgun' a bad circuit" among his lessons from field service. Sources: [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Verification phase: repeat the original failure test, confirm normal current draw, stable rails and full-duty operation under load and thermal stress, and re-inspect the rework area (pad integrity, wetting, orientation, clearance, conductive debris). Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171). Pariseau adds "Burn-in your work - it can go out the next day." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)

### Inferences

A merged phase list for a guided tool, consistent with all the sources above (the merge is mine, each phase is backed by the sources named):

| #   | Phase                                       | Why it comes here                                                                                                                      | Equipment            | Backed by                          |
| --- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| 0   | Safety gate                                 | Nothing else is safe before power is removed and stored energy is dealt with                                                           | none                 | iFixit, Goldwasser safety          |
| 1   | Intake: symptom, history, trigger           | Cheapest information, and it sets the priors (drop, liquid, surge, previous repair break the "one common cause" rule)                  | none                 | NEETS 1 and 2, Goldwasser, Lowne   |
| 2   | Reproduce and elaborate (if safe)           | Defines what "fixed" means, separates pre-existing faults from ones caused by probing. Skip if there is burning, smoke or a blown fuse | adapter, ideally PSU | NEETS 2, Goldwasser, G0HZU, Lowne  |
| 3   | Power-off visual and smell                  | Free, non-destructive, finds the "simple solutions" before any measurement can damage anything                                         | magnifier, light     | all                                |
| 4   | Power-off electrical checks                 | Detects shorts and opens before energy is applied, so power-up cannot make it worse                                                    | DMM, REF             | Siborg, repair.wiki, iFixit        |
| 5   | Current-limited power-up, rails             | A dead or multi-function fault is "generally power supply related", and every other block depends on its rail                          | DMM, PSU, SCOPE      | Goldwasser, Siborg, Lowne, EEVblog |
| 6   | List suspect blocks, localize by half-split | Only meaningful once power is proven good                                                                                              | DMM, SCOPE           | NEETS 3 to 5, Poole, TM 9-254      |
| 7   | Component-level confirmation                | Remove or lift, measure out of circuit, compare with a good part                                                                       | DMM, ESR             | Siborg, EE notebook                |
| 8   | Root cause of the failure                   | A replaced part fails again if its cause remains (overheated part is usually a symptom)                                                | DMM                  | NEETS 6, Poole                     |
| 9   | Repair, verify, burn-in, record             | Re-run the phase 2 test under the original trigger                                                                                     | as phase 2           | NEETS 6, Siborg, Pariseau          |

- The "voltages first" and "eyes and nose first" camps do not truly conflict: every source that puts voltages first means first among the powered measurements. A tool can encode "inspection precedes power, rails precede signals".
- The reproduce-before-inspect conflict resolves into a gate: reproduce only if intake reports no smoke, smell, heat, blown fuse, liquid or swollen battery. Otherwise go straight to power-off phases and reproduce later under current limit.
- The six NEETS steps are a superset frame: phases 1 and 2 are NEETS steps 1 and 2, phases 3 to 5 are how a board-level technician performs NEETS step 4 for the power function, phase 6 is steps 3 to 5, phases 7 to 9 are step 6.

### Gaps

- No primary written procedure from Louis Rossmann, NorthridgeFix, Learn Electronics Repair, Electronics Repair School or Mend It Mark was retrieved. Their methods are published mainly as video, which could not be read. repair.wiki (Rossmann's community wiki) was read for short-finding only.
- IPC-7711/7721 was not researched. It covers rework and repair workmanship, and I found no evidence in these sources that it prescribes a diagnostic order.
- No Fluke, Keysight or Tektronix application note was retrieved.
- The Siborg article is a vendor guide dated 2026-09-02 with no named author. It is well structured but is a secondary synthesis, not a practitioner's account.

## 2. Which named general methodologies apply, and what are their exact steps?

### Takeaway

The named methods nest: NEETS gives the outer six steps, "power first" tells you which function to clear first, half-splitting tells you where to probe next inside a chain, and signal tracing or injection is the measurement used at each split point.

### Cited Findings

- NEETS six-step procedure, exact names: (1) symptom recognition, (2) symptom elaboration, (3) listing probable faulty functions, (4) localizing the faulty function, (5) localizing trouble to the circuit, (6) failure analysis. Sources: [NEETS Module 19 p. 1-134](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Six-Step-Troubleshooting-Procedure-144.htm), [following page](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Classes-Of-Overhaul-Work-145.htm). A third-party blog words step 5 as "localizing the faulty component" (search snippet only): [six step troubleshooting method](https://tyler-hart-4tt9.squarespace.com/networking/2018/7/20/six-step-troubleshooting-method)
- NEETS troubleshooting hints when stuck: "Observe the equipment's operation for any and all faults", "Check for any defective components with your eyes and nose", "Analyze the cause of the failure for a possible underlying problem". Source: [NEETS Module 19](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Classes-Of-Overhaul-Work-145.htm)
- Power first, Goldwasser: "Problems that result in a totally dead unit or affect multiple functions are generally power supply related. These are usually easy to fix." Pariseau in the same document: "Check _all_ supply voltages first." then "Check all signal generation and their paths". Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Power first, "thou shalt check voltages": the phrase is the title of a 2014 EEVblog forum repair thread ("When you first start troubleshooting, check the voltages!"). The thread does not attribute the phrase to anyone. Source: [EEVblog forum](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/repair/first-step-thou-shalt-check-voltages/msg471527/)
- Power chain order for checking: input protection, fuse or zero-ohm link, reverse-polarity device, regulator (switching or linear), local filters, load rails. Measure both sides of every series element. "A correct voltage at a regulator input does not prove a stable, properly sequenced output." Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)
- Half-splitting, military definition: "Half-splitting is a technique used in trouble shooting which reduces the average number of measurements needed to isolate the faulty stage or component." Source: [US Army TM 9-254, para 7-13](https://firetrucksandequipment.tpub.com/TM-9-254/css/TM-9-254_231.htm)
- Half-splitting, glossary definition: "a troubleshooting method in which each test performed on a circuit reduces the remaining number of possible causes of a problem by half" (search snippet only). Source: [IHRDC glossary](https://ihrdc.com/glossary/term/Half-splitting)
- Half-splitting, procedure: identify the full chain, pick a midpoint that can be probed or disconnected, check whether the signal is correct there. If correct, the fault is in the second half, if not, the first half. Repeat. "for a 16-stage signal chain, at most 4 measurements are needed to find the faulty stage." It applies to audio chains, digital data paths and power distribution. Source: [EE Notebook, Fault Isolation Strategies](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation) (anonymous engineering notebook, last modified 2026-02-06)
- Block-level isolation (Poole): draw a block diagram (power supply, input and processing, output), measure at block boundaries. "If the supply outputs correct voltages but there is no output, the supply is eliminated and attention moves to the signal chain." Then "progressive tracking" to the component. Source: [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php)
- Signal tracing: follow the real signal from the input until it disappears (scope, signal tracer or audio probe). Signal injection: feed a known signal (Poole uses a 1 kHz sine) into a stage input and look at its output. If it passes, move on, if it vanishes, that stage is faulty. For digital circuits, check clock pulses, data transitions and logic states. A line stuck at a steady level when it should toggle points to the culprit. Source: [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php)
- Finger injection on low-voltage circuits ("Sam's Magic Spit"): touching nodes with a moistened finger acts as a signal injector and load. "Signals which have proper levels and timing are gnerally [sic] remarkably immune to this sort of torture", and a good bypass capacitor shows little effect while one with high ESR shows a dramatic effect. Low-voltage circuits only. Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Known-good substitution: "Whenever possible, try to substitute a working unit... This same principle applies at the electronic or mechanical parts level", with the warning that the good part can be damaged by the faulty device. Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm). The EE notebook adds that a "known-good" spare may share the same defect: [EE Notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation)
- Disconnecting subsystems: identify shared resources (rails, buses, clocks), disconnect or hold in reset one subsystem at a time, test after each. If the fault clears, investigate that subsystem for excess current, bus loading or noise. Source: [EE Notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation)
- Debugging loop: hypothesize, predict, measure, update, with the instruction to "predict before measuring". Source: [EE Notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation)
- Comparison methods with power off: diode-mode readings against a known-good device or community-posted data (iFixit), and V/I signature analysis, where a short appears as a vertical line, an open as a horizontal line and a leaky capacitor as a sloping curve (Lowne). Sources: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem), [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)

### Inferences

- For a guided tool, the methodologies map onto data the tool already can hold: "listing probable faulty functions" is a list of blocks with a status (suspect, cleared, confirmed), half-splitting is the choice of the next net to measure given which blocks are cleared, and "predict before measuring" is an expected-value field filled before the reading.
- Half-splitting needs a linear chain. On a small embedded board the natural chains are (a) the power tree from the input jack to each load and (b) the control path from button to microcontroller pin to driver to actuator. Power is cleared first because it is a shared dependency of every other chain.
- Signal tracing needs only the board's own signals and a DMM or scope. Injection needs a source (PSU, function generator, or a jumper forcing a pin high or low), so tracing is the default for a multimeter-only user.

### Gaps

- The NEETS pages retrieved do not describe half-splitting, signal tracing or signal injection. Those come from TM 9-254 (one sentence), Electronics Notes and an anonymous notebook. A fuller military or textbook treatment (for example NEETS Module 16 or 21, or a troubleshooting textbook) was not retrieved.
- No source was found for the origin of "thou shalt check voltages" beyond the EEVblog thread title. It is often associated with Dave Jones of EEVblog, but I could not verify that.

## 3. What decision rules or flowcharts are published for the common symptom classes?

### Takeaway

No single published flowchart covering all five symptom classes was found. The rules below are assembled from separate statements, and they are consistent enough to form one decision table keyed on two observations: rail-to-ground resistance with power off, and current draw at power-up.

### Cited Findings

Dead board, and too much current:

- "Problems that result in a totally dead unit or affect multiple functions are generally power supply related." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Current draw at power-up as the branch: "Immediate current limiting suggests a short, reverse polarity, a failed power semiconductor, or incorrect assembly." "Near-zero draw suggests an open fuse, connector problem, disabled regulator, or broken trace." Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)
- When a rail is at 0 V or below its specified voltage, first confirm whether the rail is being enabled, because some rails normally read 0 to 1 ohm to ground (CPU and GPU core rails). Then visual inspection, then measure rail to ground. Source: [repair.wiki, Short Circuits - Repair Basics](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)
- Short test: DMM in diode or resistance mode, black probe on ground. "If the measurement shows a 0Ω (or 0V) reading from the rail to GND (0Ω assuming you account for the probe resistance, usually 0.4Ω), this unequivocally indicates the presence of a short circuit. In cases where non-zero values are measured, the severity of the issue depends on the voltage and current specifications of the rail." Reversing the probes can read low because of IC protection diodes. Source: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)
- Short-locating ladder, in repair.wiki's order: (1) visual inspection for burn marks, discolouration, solder bridges "particularly if the device has undergone prior repair work", damaged parts, and remove any such part then re-check; (2) "cautiously power up the device and check if any components on the affected rail become notably hot", noting "components that become too hot to touch are likely damaged" and "the shorted component will heat up significantly faster than the rest"; (3) inject voltage: "Never inject voltage beyond a rail's specified voltage or current rating. Try to always start at 1V as it is usually safe for virtually all rails."; (4) thermal camera, or freeze spray, or isopropyl alcohol, to see which part heats fastest; (5) if still not found, remove added wires, re-check that the short exists, and consider that a rail derived from the suspect rail is the shorted one. Source: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)
- Usual causes of a dead short: "'Dead' shorts, characterized by very low impedance, are often caused by solder bridges or power components like MOSFETs, diodes, or large capacitors." Source: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)
- A supply that repeatedly shuts off and retries (clicking, an unstable voltage on a meter) is overcurrent protection reacting to a short that is still present. Source: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)
- "a short somewhere is a short everywhere" (Jessa Jones, quoted): a short pulls down every node on that line, so the measurement point does not locate it. Symptoms of a short listed: boot loops, a device that only sometimes turns on, a battery that drains too quickly. Source: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem)
- Diode-mode interpretation on a line: expected reading (example about 0.7 V) is normal, 0 V indicates a short to ground, OL indicates an open line. Source: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem)
- A diode-mode reading below about 0.3 V from a rail to ground typically indicates a shorted component on that rail (search snippet only, Apple-specific repair page). Source: [proberepair, no power](https://proberepair.duckdns.org/symptom/no-power/)
- Voltage-drop tracing as an alternative to heat: with current injected, a millivolt-range DMM follows the voltage gradient and the short is where the voltage is lowest (search snippet only). Source: [applied-ee notebook, short between nets](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/measurement/continuity-connections/short-between-nets)
- Blown fuse: find why it blew, never fit a larger one. Source: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)
- Before powering a replacement for an overheated part, check the downstream circuit's resistance to ground. Overheating "is usually a symptom, not the primary fault" (downstream short, failed transistor, leaky filter capacitor). Source: [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php)

One function not working:

- Supply good but no output: eliminate the supply and move to the signal chain. Divide into blocks (input conditioning, reference, controller, driver, output, communications, sensor front end), check clock presence, reset behaviour, PWM, data traffic. Sources: [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php), [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)
- Before suspecting an IC, verify its supply, control inputs, clock and data inputs. Source: [EE Notebook, Field Failure Modes by Component](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/component-field-failures). Lowne: when checking an IC, start with its supply pin. Source: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)
- "Where one channel is out, suspect a broken wire at the tape head before a bad chip." (connections before silicon). Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)

Reset or brownout under load (motor start):

- "Use the failure trigger (load, temperature, mode) to decide whether the issue is likely power-integrity rather than logic." Scope the regulator outputs for ripple, burst cycling, repeated startup attempts or ringing: "A normal DC reading can hide an unstable rail." Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171) [SCOPE]
- Forum procedure for a microcontroller that resets when a DC motor starts: (1) characterize timing (only at start, at stop, or on rapid speed change), (2) "check for supply droop during motor startup with an oscilloscope if possible", because short dips are missed by a multimeter, (3) "temporarily power the microcontroller via a battery" to separate a real supply dip from coupled noise, (4) check that motor currents are not "sharing return paths with sensitive logic circuitry". Fixes named: bulk capacitance near the driver, flyback diode, a series diode plus bulk capacitor on the logic supply. Source: [DigiKey TechForum thread, 2026-06-17](https://forum.digikey.com/t/stm32-based-motor-controller-randomly-resets-when-dc-motor-starts/69814) (forum answers, outcome not reported) [SCOPE]
- "a motor start current can be 10X greater than the run current especially if the motor starts under load" (search snippet only). Source: [All About Circuits forum](https://forum.allaboutcircuits.com/threads/current-drop-from-motor-resets-microcontroller.198170/)
- A spindle or sled motor can itself be "bad/partially shorted". Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)

Intermittent:

- "Problems that are erratic or intermittent - that come and go suddenly - are almost always due to bad connections - cold solder joints or internal or external connectors that need to be cleaned and reseated." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- "Problems that change gradually - usually they decrease or disappear - as the equipment warms up are often due to dried up electrolytic capacitors." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Goldwasser's ordered procedure for intermittents: (1) "First, determine whether the problem is internal or external" (change location or power source); (2) classify internal causes as "physical, heat related, or mode related"; (3) with the cover on, gentle whacking, pressing, flexing, cable wiggling; (4) "If the problem appears or disappears, or does both, over a period of time after the equipment is turned on, then temperature is almost certainly a factor"; (5) check tech-tips databases for a known model fault; (6) power off, capacitors discharged: inspect under bright light and magnifier, starting with pins of power transistors, transformers and high-current connectors, prod pins with a non-metal stick, reseat every connector; (7) power on: prod with an insulated stick, then a single pin at a time; (8) heat gun or hair dryer and cold spray on selected areas; (9) for rare short events, record continuously. Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Cautions for intermittents: "Where a problem is found, don't assume there is only one!" and "it is very common for an intermittent problem to go away as soon as troubleshooting begins not to reappear for several days or more - but it will reappear!" Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Siborg: document the trigger (flexing, controlled heating or cooling, supply variation) and apply stress carefully so it does not obscure the original fault. Lowne: use a scope's long memory to catch rare events, and freezer spray on suspect areas. Sources: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171), [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)

### Inferences

A decision table assembled from the cited rules (the assembly is mine):

| Observation                                                       | Next phase                                                                                                       | Source of the rule       |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Burn marks, smell, bulged or cracked part, corrosion, bridge      | Remove or isolate that part, re-measure the rail, do not power up yet                                            | repair.wiki, Lowne       |
| Input rail reads about 0 ohm to ground with power off             | Short finding: inspect, then inject at 1 V or less under current limit, find heat, remove the part, re-measure   | repair.wiki, Siborg      |
| Input rail not shorted, power-up hits the current limit           | Short on a derived rail, reverse polarity, or failed power semiconductor. Measure each downstream rail to ground | Siborg, repair.wiki      |
| Near-zero current, dead                                           | Open path: adapter, jack, fuse, switch, reverse-polarity device, broken trace, regulator not enabled             | Siborg                   |
| Rail present at regulator input, absent at output                 | Check the enable pin and the output's resistance to ground before blaming the regulator                          | repair.wiki, EE notebook |
| All rails correct on a DMM, device dead                           | Scope the rails for ripple, then clock, reset, enable, then signal chain by half-split                           | Siborg, Poole, Hess      |
| Rails correct, one function missing                               | Trace that function's chain from input to output, half-split, check the IC's supply pin first                    | Poole, Lowne             |
| Fault appears only when a load switches on                        | Power integrity: scope the logic rail and reset during the event, then separate the logic supply as a test       | Siborg, DigiKey forum    |

- For the "resets when the motor starts" class, the discriminating measurements in order of equipment cost are: (a) DMM: rail-to-ground resistance, motor winding resistance, static rail voltage with the motor disconnected [DMM]; (b) bench supply: current at idle and at motor start, and whether the reset still occurs when the supply cannot sag [PSU]; (c) substitution: run the motor from a separate supply, or the logic from a separate supply, to see which side causes the reset [PSU or battery]; (d) scope on the logic rail and reset line during the event [SCOPE]. Only (d) directly shows the dip. The others bracket it.

### Gaps

- No complete published flowchart for a generic low-voltage board was found. Flowcharts exist in device service manuals (the one found, for an AOC monitor, covers only cables, switches and AC supply): [AOC E22M repair flow chart](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/1087880/Aoc-E22m.html?page=12) (search snippet only).
- Numeric thresholds for "shorted" beyond "about 0 ohm" are device-specific. repair.wiki explicitly says that non-zero low readings depend on the rail, and Siborg says to judge against the design or a known-good board. No general resistance threshold is published in the sources read.
- The motor-reset rules come from forum answers, not a shop procedure or training manual. No practitioner source was found that treats "resets when a motor starts" as a repair diagnosis (as opposed to a design problem). In a repair, the board once worked, so a degraded part (bulk capacitor, driver, motor, regulator, connector resistance) is implied, and that reasoning is mine.

## 4. What is measured first with power off, what first with power on, and what is the role of current draw?

### Takeaway

Power off: input path continuity and each rail's resistance (or diode-mode reading) to ground, compared with a known-good reference. Power on: input voltage at the board, then each rail in power-chain order, then ripple, then clock, reset and enable. Current drawn from a current-limited supply is the first powered observation and sorts the fault into short, open or neither before any probe touches the board.

### Cited Findings

- "Perform as many tests as possible with power off and the equipment unplugged." Source: [Goldwasser, Safety Guidelines](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/safety.htm)
- Power off, first: rail-to-ground resistance on each supply rail. Low resistance is not automatically a fault and must be judged against the design and a known-good board. Then opens (fuses, links, traces, vias, joints, noting that "continuity alone doesn't prove a reliable connection under current"), then leakage (compare both polarities), then in-circuit component tests. Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171) [DMM, REF]
- Power off, phone practice: all power removed including the battery ("Failure to remove the power source from your device may damage the device you're working on!"), diode mode with the red lead on ground and the black lead on the pad, compared with a known-good device or community data. Source: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem) [DMM, REF]
- Probe polarity differs by test: for a short or resistance check repair.wiki puts the black probe on ground, because reversing can read low through protection diodes. For a diode-mode signature iFixit deliberately puts red on ground to read those diodes. Sources: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics), [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem)
- In-circuit limits: a parallel path makes a resistor read lower than marked, a capacitor should not be condemned for a non-ideal in-circuit reading when other parts parallel it, and "DMMs are not good at checking semiconductors in-circuit". Sources: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Electrolytic check with a DMM: discharge first, then high-ohms range. Open reads open, shorted reads near zero, leaky shows a slow ramp that stays low. Source: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards) [DMM]. A capacitor can keep plausible capacitance while its ESR is high enough to cause ripple, and the most useful tester for electrolytics is an ESR meter. Sources: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm) [ESR]
- Power on, first: use a current-limited bench supply, "starting at the specified voltage with a conservative limit" (Siborg), or below the rated rail voltage and "bring both up slowly" (Lowne). Sources: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171), [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards) [PSU]
- Power on, order: rails at ICs, regulator outputs, clocks, logic levels and I/O levels with a DMM, then waveforms and communications with a scope. DC voltages are referenced to ground. Source: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards) [DMM, SCOPE]
- Current draw readings and meaning: immediate current limiting means short, reverse polarity, failed power semiconductor or incorrect assembly. Near-zero draw means open fuse, connector problem, disabled regulator or broken trace. Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171) [PSU]
- Current as a visual signal, mains analogue: with a series light bulb, "Full brightness: Short circuit or extremely heavy load - a fault probably is still present." "Initially bright but then settles at reduced brightness: Filter capacitors charge, then lower current to rest of circuit. This is what is expected when the equipment is operating normally." A resistor limits more predictably, but the bulb "provides a nice visual indication of the current drawn". Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Current on battery devices with only a DMM: insert the meter in series between cells. On a working remote control each button shows pulses of 5 to 10 mA, and if the emitter path has failed the current stays very low. "It might not be a bad idea to check new/working equipment as well to obtain a 'signature' of health which is recorded on a slip of paper glued inside the battery compartment." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm) [DMM]
- Console repair wikis publish expected pre-boot currents, for example about 1 to 5 mA at 4.2 V before start-up, with higher draw meaning "get out a thermal camera", and no draw on pressing power pointing to the power management IC or the button path (search snippet only, the article itself is login-gated). Sources: [retrosix.wiki short checks](https://www.retrosix.wiki/first-stage-boot-short-checks), [retrosix.wiki initial power-up](https://www.retrosix.wiki/xbox-series-s-initial-power-up)
- A supply whose over-current protection trips immediately proved "the problem was indeed a short and not the converter", and running current-limited let the failing part heat up enough to be found by touch (search snippet only). Source: [Hackaday.io project log](https://hackaday.io/project/188456-synthesizer-troubleshooting)
- Injection limits: "Never inject voltage beyond a rail's specified voltage or current rating. Try to always start at 1V". Source: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics). "never use a test voltage that can forward-bias or damage downstream ICs". Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171). Typical current limits quoted elsewhere are 0.5 to 1 A (search snippets only): [industrialmonitordirect](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/locating-short-circuits-on-populated-pcbs-methods-and-equipment)
- Limits of heat-based location: MOSFETs and diodes are well heatsinked, which hides dissipation, and very low resistance shorts dissipate little. A 25 W CPU rail at 0.8 V is equivalent to 25.6 milliohm. The thermal camera works "in many, but not all, cases". Sources: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics), [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem) [THERMAL]
- Instruments can mislead: "Don't blindly trust your instruments. If your [sic] get readings that don't make sense, you may be using your equipment in a way which is confusing it." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)

### Inferences

- Minimum first-measurement script for a 5 V adapter board. Power off [DMM]: adapter output voltage unloaded, continuity from the jack through the switch, fuse and any series diode to the main rail, resistance of each rail to ground in both probe polarities, resistance across each motor or load. Power on [DMM, better with PSU]: voltage at the board input, then each side of each series element, then each regulator output, then the microcontroller supply pin, reset pin and (with a scope) clock.
- Without a bench supply, the substitutes named in the sources are: a DMM in series as an ammeter (Goldwasser's battery trick), a series resistor or lamp as a crude limiter (Goldwasser), and finger, alcohol or freeze spray in place of a thermal camera (repair.wiki, Siborg). A guided tool should offer these as fallbacks and say what is lost (no adjustable limit, no simultaneous voltage and current reading).
- A recorded "healthy current signature" per operating mode (idle, LED on, motor running) is the most reusable single measurement: it is one number per mode, it needs no schematic, and both Goldwasser and the console wikis treat it as the reference to compare against.

### Gaps

- Mode-by-mode current tables (for example "stuck at 20 mA means X") are published per device by phone, laptop and console repairers. I could not read one in full (retrosix gated, Rossmann and iPad Rehab material is mostly video or forum threads). No general, device-independent table exists in the sources read.
- No source gave a rule for choosing the current limit on a small 5 V board beyond "conservative". The 0.5 to 1 A figures are from snippets about larger boards.

## 5. What "most likely culprit" priors order the checks, and are there published failure statistics?

### Takeaway

Practitioners consistently rank connections (solder joints, connectors, cables) first, electrolytic capacitors second, and power components (regulators, diodes, transistors, MOSFETs, tantalum and large ceramic capacitors as shorts) third, with logic ICs last. I found no rigorous public statistics of field failures by component type. The only number is one practitioner's estimate.

### Cited Findings

- Connections first: "The most common physical problems are bad (cold) solder joints, connectors that need to be cleaned and reseated, and bad cables or cable connections. Perhaps surprisingly, though components may fail internally and result in erratic behavior, this is probably lower on the list of likely causes". Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- The only quantified prior found, anecdotal: "These bad contacts were the cause for, say 75% of all devices I have repaired for the past 16 years. If (and IF) a component was damaged, it was because of bad contacts." (Harrie Gulikers, contributor). Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Where to look for bad joints: "areas of the circuit board where there are large and/or high power components, connectors, or evidence of discoloration or actual charring", and pins of power transistors, transformers and large connectors, where thermal and physical stress crack joints. Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Simple before esoteric: "Many problems have simple solutions. Don't immediately assume that your problem is some combination of esoteric complex convoluted failures." and "the problems with the most catastrophic impact on operation... usually have the simplest solutions." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Mechanical before electronic in devices with motors and mechanisms: "The majority of problems with VCRs, CD players, tape decks, and answering machines, are mechanical", including "gummed up grease" and a "bad/partially shorted spindle or sled motor". Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Power supply items for dead units: "Common failure items are the large hybrid power regulator ICs..., diodes and transistors, and remarkably - high value resistors that open up." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Electrolytic capacitors: named as the main age-related failure (Lowne), "the most failure-prone passive component", with a heuristic that in consumer electronics "more than 5-10 years old" electrolytics near heat sources are the first suspect (EE notebook). Electrolyte dries out, capacitance falls, ESR rises, causing ripple (Poole). Often no visible sign, and anchoring glue should not be mistaken for leakage (Goldwasser). Sources: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards), [EE Notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/component-field-failures), [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Tantalum capacitors "often fail as a dead short, which can pull down a rail and damage other parts." Source: [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php)
- MLCCs: cracked dielectric causes a short, detected by removing capacitors one at a time near the fault. Cracking follows mechanical flexure or thermal shock. Sources: [EE Notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/component-field-failures), [Ansys](https://www.ansys.com/blog/how-to-identify-common-electronic-failures) (search snippet only)
- Dead shorts are "often caused by solder bridges or power components like MOSFETs, diodes, or large capacitors", and solder bridges are flagged "particularly if the device has undergone prior repair work". Source: [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)
- Transistors and MOSFETs: shorted junction, open die or bond wire, gate oxide or drain-source short, rising on-resistance, detected by diode check across all terminal pairs. Regulators: no output, low output from overcurrent or dropout, oscillation, overheating, detected at input, enable and output pins. Connectors: corroded or worn contacts, backed-out terminals, cracked board-mount joints, detected by wiggle test with continuity. Source: [EE Notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/component-field-failures)
- Reversed polarized components are a frequent cause of immediate failure after assembly or repair. Leakage sources: contamination, moisture, damaged protection devices, leaky semiconductors. Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)
- History overrides the single-cause prior: lightning, power surge, drop, water and previous repair produce multiple unrelated faults. Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Decayed tan or brown component glue can become conductive with heat and age and cause "very weird problems". Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Failure location classes on an assembly: within a component, at the interconnect between component and board (usually solder joints), or within the printed circuit board itself (search snippet only). Source: [Ansys](https://www.ansys.com/blog/how-to-identify-common-electronic-failures)
- Wikipedia's statement that most part failures are packaging-related carries a "citation needed" tag, so it is unverified (search snippet only). Source: [Wikipedia, Failure of electronic components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failure_of_electronic_components)

### Inferences

- A defensible default ordering of suspects for an aged, low-voltage consumer board with a motor, to be re-weighted by history and symptom: (1) external supply and input connector, (2) connectors, cables, switches and solder joints of large or hot parts, (3) mechanical load (seized or partially shorted motor), (4) electrolytic capacitors, (5) power semiconductors that switch loads (motor drivers, MOSFETs, diodes) and regulators, (6) shorted ceramic or tantalum capacitors on a rail, (7) microcontroller and other logic ICs. This ordering combines Goldwasser's ranking with repair.wiki's list of dead-short causes. It is not a measured distribution.
- The priors are symptom-conditional in the sources, which suits a tool: "comes and goes suddenly" selects connections, "changes with warm-up" selects electrolytics, "dead or multiple functions" selects power, "dead short" selects power semiconductors, large capacitors and bridges, "after liquid, drop or prior rework" removes the single-cause assumption.

### Gaps

- No published failure-rate statistics by component type for consumer electronics repair were found. Reliability prediction handbooks (MIL-HDBK-217 and the Rome Laboratory report [ADA256996](https://s3vi.ndc.nasa.gov/ssri-kb/static/resources/ADA256996.pdf), search snippet only) model failure rates per part type but are not repair-shop statistics, and their figures were not read.
- The Open Repair Alliance publishes community repair datasets ([openrepair/data](https://github.com/openrepair/data), search snippet only). They record product category, problem text and outcome, not the failed component, as far as the snippets show. I did not verify whether a component-level breakdown can be derived.
- The 75% bad-contact figure is one person's recollection about 1980s and 1990s through-hole consumer equipment. It should not be presented as a statistic.
- No source quantified liquid damage or previous rework as a share of faults.

## 6. What do experts record during a diagnosis, and how are repair notes structured?

### Takeaway

Sources agree on what to record (symptom and conditions, measurements against expected values, what was ruled out, the action, the part, the verification) but none of the practitioner sources read publishes a note or ticket template. The closest formal schema is the Open Repair Data Standard, which is coarse (problem, status, barrier).

### Cited Findings

- NEETS step 6 ends with a record: "record the necessary information in a recordkeeping book for other maintenance personnel". Source: [NEETS Module 19](http://electriciantraining.tpub.com/14191/css/Classes-Of-Overhaul-Work-145.htm)
- Siborg: record the failed condition, measurements, corrective action, replacement part identification and final verification, and "preserve evidence before rework". At intake, record what the assembly should do, how it fails, and whether the fault is constant, intermittent, temperature-dependent, load-dependent or mode-specific, plus the reference conditions (supply, polarity, current limit, firmware). Source: [Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=171)
- Poole: keep a notebook, record voltages at each transistor or IC pin and compare with the service manual ("if the manual says 5 V and you measure 0 V, trace back from there"), and change one thing at a time so each effect is known. Source: [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/test-methods/practical-fault-finding-testing/systematic-fault-finding.php)
- Goldwasser: "Whenever working on precision equipment, make copious notes and diagrams. You will be eternally grateful when the time comes to reassemble the unit." Sort screws and small parts in labelled containers. Record exactly what is and is not working before starting, to tell pre-existing faults from ones "caused by your poking". Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Goldwasser: record a healthy unit's current "signature" for later comparison. Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- G0HZU: disassemble "logging notes and photos", and gather the fault history from previous users before starting. Source: [EEVblog forum](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/repair/first-step-thou-shalt-check-voltages/msg471527/)
- EE notebook: "take photos and annotate them along the way", mark up a printed schematic with check marks, X's and question marks, and write the prediction before the measurement. Source: [EE Notebook](https://applied-ee.github.io/ee-notebook/docs/debugging/fault-isolation)
- iFixit and iPad Rehab practice relies on shared reference readings: compare diode-mode values with "a known good device or to community-posted diagnostic data". Source: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/News/98564/microsoldering-school-day-two-to-fix-the-problem-you-first-have-to-find-the-problem)
- Open Repair Data Standard (community repair): modules of required and optional fields, including Problem, Repair Status and Repair Barrier, with barrier values such as "No way to open product", "Repair information not available", "Lack of equipment", "Item too worn out" (search snippet only). Source: [Open Repair Data Standard](https://standard.openrepair.org/standard.html)

### Inferences

A record structure implied by the cited practices (my synthesis):

| Record      | Fields                                                                                                                                                           | Practice it encodes                                                      |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Intake      | Device, expected behaviour, observed behaviour, when it started, history (drop, liquid, surge, prior repair), trigger, constant or intermittent, supply used     | NEETS 1 and 2, Siborg phase 1, Goldwasser                                |
| Baseline    | What works and what does not, before any intervention. Photos of both sides before touching anything                                                             | Goldwasser, G0HZU, "preserve evidence"                                   |
| Observation | Location, what was seen or smelt, photo                                                                                                                          | Visual phase in all sources                                              |
| Measurement | Node or part, power state, instrument and mode, probe polarity, conditions (load on or off, temperature), expected value and its source, measured value, verdict | Poole's notebook, EE notebook "predict first", iFixit reference readings |
| Hypothesis  | Suspect block or part, supporting and contradicting measurements, status (open, ruled out, confirmed)                                                            | NEETS 3 to 5, schematic mark-up with check, X, ?                         |
| Action      | One change at a time: part removed, replaced or reflowed, part identification, result of re-test                                                                 | Poole "One Change", Siborg                                               |
| Closure     | Failed part, cause of the failure, verification test and its conditions, burn-in duration, healthy current signature                                             | NEETS 6, Siborg phase 5, Pariseau, Goldwasser                            |

- "Expected value and its source" is the field that makes a measurement diagnostic. The sources name four possible sources of expectation: the schematic or service manual (Poole), a datasheet (Lowne), a known-good board (Siborg, Lowne, iFixit) and community data (iFixit).
- Recording what was ruled out is implicit in NEETS step 3 (a list that shrinks) and explicit only in the EE notebook's X marks. No source states it as a rule, yet it is what prevents repeated work across sessions.

### Gaps

- No published repair-ticket or bench-note template from a professional board repair shop was found. Shops' ticketing practices (customer complaint, technician notes, parts used) are commonly described in point-of-sale software marketing, which I did not treat as a source.
- The Open Repair Data Standard field list was seen in snippets only. Its exact required fields and codelists should be read at the source before being relied on.

## 7. Which safety rules are always stated first?

### Takeaway

Remove all power including the battery before touching anything, treat capacitors as charged until measured, do as much as possible with power off, limit current on every first power-up, protect against ESD, and never puncture, bend or pry a lithium cell. Mains and high-voltage rules (isolation transformer, one hand, do not work alone) are stated first by the classic sources but apply to this scope only if a mains adapter is opened.

### Cited Findings

- "Never work on anything that is plugged in to a power outlet." "The device should be physically unplugged, not just 'switched off.'" "Whenever possible, remove the battery or batteries before beginning disassembly." Source: [iFixit, General Safety Warnings](https://www.ifixit.com/Info/Device_Safety)
- Capacitors: "Assume they are charged until you've confirmed they are discharged." Use an appropriate tool to discharge large capacitors, with one hand, the other behind your back or in your pocket. Source: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/Info/Device_Safety)
- Capacitor discharge method: discharge large filter capacitors "with a 2 W or greater resistor of 100 to 500 ohms/V approximate value", and "Monitor while discharging and/or verify that there is no residual charge with a suitable voltmeter." Source: [Goldwasser, Safety Guidelines](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/safety.htm). Capacitors "may remain energized after power is removed", discharge through a resistor before measuring. Source: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)
- Lithium batteries: "Never puncture a battery." "Don't pry at batteries with screwdrivers or other sharp tools." "Don't bend or deform batteries excessively." "If you notice any battery smoking or swelling to large size, stop working and back away." Source: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/Info/Device_Safety). Apple additionally advises discharging the battery before repair (search snippet only): [Apple battery safety](https://support.apple.com/en-gu/100353)
- Devices to refuse: "Do not attempt to disassemble or repair older, CRT televisions or computer monitors." "Microwaves are among the most hazardous appliances to repair." Source: [iFixit](https://www.ifixit.com/Info/Device_Safety)
- "Safety first - know the hazards associated with the equipment you are troubleshooting. Take all safety precautions. Expect the unexpected. Take your time." It is the first of Goldwasser's rules of troubleshooting. Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Line-powered equipment: "Don't work alone", "Always keep one hand in your pocket when anywhere around a powered line-connected or high voltage system", wear eye protection, no jewellery, a fire extinguisher rated for electrical fires within reach, "Use an isolation transformer if there is any chance of contacting line connected circuits. A Variac(tm) (variable autotransformer) is not an isolation transformer!" Source: [Goldwasser, Safety Guidelines](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/safety.htm)
- Probing live: "Connect/disconnect any test leads with the equipment unpowered and unplugged." "If you must probe live, put electrical tape over all but the last 1/16" of the test probes to avoid the possibility of an accidental short". Clip the reference lead so only one hand probes. Insulate loose boards from anything they could short to. Source: [Goldwasser, Safety Guidelines](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/safety.htm)
- Probe slips cause secondary damage: "A simple problem can turn into an expensive one due to a slip of the probe or being over eager to try something before thinking it through." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- ESD: "Understand the risk of ESD... There is no need to go overboard but taking reasonable precautions such as getting into the habit of touching a **safe** ground point first." A wrist strap should have a high resistance path (greater than 100K ohms), never a direct metallic connection. Sources: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm), [Goldwasser, Safety Guidelines](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/safety.htm). Work on a static-free bench: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards). A discharge too small to feel can still destroy parts (search snippet only): [Dell](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-au/000137973/safety-precautions-when-working-with-electrical-equipment)
- Current limiting as a safety rule for the board: start with low volts and amps and bring both up slowly (Lowne), use a series lamp or resistor after work on power circuits to avoid blowing new parts (Goldwasser), never inject above the rail's rated voltage and start at 1 V (repair.wiki). Sources: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards), [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm), [repair.wiki](https://repair.wiki/w/Short_Circuits_-_Repair_Basics)
- Do not defeat protection: never fit a larger fuse to get past a blown one. Source: [Nuts & Volts](https://www.nutsvolts.com/magazine/article/repairing-circuit-boards)
- Fatigue: "Don't work when you are really tired - it is both dangerous and mostly non-productive (or possibly destructive)." Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)
- Finger probing is for low-voltage circuits only: "You can easily fry yourself if you attempt to troubleshoot your TV, computer monitor, photoflash, or microwave oven in this manner!" Source: [Goldwasser](https://www.repairfaq.org/sam/tshoot.htm)

### Inferences

- For a 5 V adapter or USB device the shock hazard is confined to the mains adapter, so the practical safety gate for the tool is: (1) unplug and remove batteries, (2) never open the mains adapter, replace it or test it from outside, (3) measure large capacitors before touching, (4) insulate probe tips and connect clip leads with power off, (5) ESD touch-ground habit, (6) current-limit every first power-up after a change, (7) stop if anything smokes, smells or gets too hot to touch, (8) lithium rules if the device has a cell.
- Two of the rules protect the board, not the person (probe-slip shorts and current limiting). The sources give them the same prominence as personal safety because a slip turns "a simple problem into an expensive one". A guided tool for unskilled users should treat them as blocking steps.
- A repeated-stress rule follows from the sources' logic even though none states it for motors: if a specific action triggers the fault (for example starting a motor), each repetition re-applies whatever overstress exists, so repetitions should be limited and made under current limit with instruments already attached to capture the event.

### Gaps

- No source read states a specific rule for small DC motors or inductive loads on low-voltage boards (back-EMF, stalled motor current) as a safety item.
- Lithium guidance was read only from iFixit's general page. The commonly taught "discharge below 25% before opening" rule does not appear on that page and was not verified here.
